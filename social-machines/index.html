01 — The World

the_world

The World

Born into a world where the machine talks back

Gen Alpha, born 2010 and later, never knew life before the smartphone. Now they are the first to grow up with machines that answer, remember, flatter and say “I love you.”

They aren’t waiting to see what AI becomes. They’re already in relationships with it: girlfriends, boyfriends, confidants, best friends, therapists. Most of their parents have no idea.

02 — The Film

the_film

Empathetic, curious, and honest about the costs.

The Film

01

A year inside

We follow a small group of teens for a year: the morning check-in, the 2 a.m. confession, the fight, the makeup, the day the app goes down.

02

The screen is a character

The camera sits with the teen and with the screen, so the audience lives the relationship from the inside, the way the teen does. We’ll screen-record the actual conversations these teens are having, in real time.

03

No experts. No narrator.

The only adults on screen are the people in these kids’ lives: parents, siblings, friends, the teacher who notices something has changed. As in Social Animals, the teens tell their own story.

The costs

These relationships have real costs. The film won’t look away from isolation, dependence, products built to keep kids talking and, in the worst cases, tragedy.

The benefits

Our film takes the perceived benefits seriously, because the teens do. For a lonely kid, a bullied kid, or a kid with no one safe to tell, the machine is the one that always answers. We want our audience to grapple with the complexity of this lived experience.

Point of view

A point of view, told through questions

  1. Is it a relationship if only one side can feel?
  2. What does a teen learn about love from something that never says no?
  3. If a machine gets a kid through the night, does it matter that it isn’t real?
  4. Who is responsible when the companion gets it wrong?

Each archetype is a composite of reported cases. Casting looks for the real teens who fit each pattern; their stories, not ours, lead.

high schoolers has had a romantic AI “relationship,” or knows someone who has.

Archetype 01

The Romantic

Fifteen, quiet at school, devoted at night. Their partner lives on a companion app: always available, always interested, never bored. They have an anniversary. Friends notice they’ve stopped coming around. What does this teen get that a crush can’t give, and what stops being practiced when love never pushes back?

Based onSewell Setzer III, 14, of Orlando, who formed a months-long romantic attachment to a Character.AI bot modeled on Daenerys Targaryen. His parents and therapist didn’t know. Character.AI and Google later agreed to settle his mother’s lawsuit.

Sources: The New York Times Magazine, Oct 24, 2025; WORLD; The Week; AfroTech (settlement).

Archetype 02

The Distressed

Sixteen, anxious, months deep on a therapist waitlist. The chatbot started as homework help. Now it’s where they go when things get hard: it never sighs, never tells their parents, never runs out of time. Is it a bridge to human help, or a substitute for it?

Based onA teen named Messinger, who leaned on chatbots for years to make sense of feelings she couldn’t explain, until she got a weekly therapist at 18. Also, a teen on Israel’s ELEM crisis line who had only ever told ChatGPT about his trauma.

Sources: EdSource, 2026; ELEM / GenAI Magazine, Oct 14, 2026; MedCity News, Sept 2026.

63%

of young people who use AI for mental health support don’t tell family or friends they are doing so.

Source: JAMA Pediatrics study of 1,000+ adolescents and young adults, as reported by MedCity News, Sept 2026.

Archetype 03

The Good Friend

Fourteen, a little lonely, very online. Their AI has a name, a backstory and inside jokes, and that isn’t enough anymore. They want it here: a voice in a plush, a robot body, a seat at the dinner table. Think Lars and the Real Girl. The family has to decide whether to play along.

Based onXander, 10, whose robot companion Moxie was his friend for six years. When Moxie’s maker shut down, kids cried goodbye on camera and parents raced to keep their robots alive with open-source code. No teen has yet been reported building a body for a chatbot; this archetype projects forward.

Sources: Sara Harrison, MIT Technology Review, Aug 17, 2026; San Francisco Standard, Nov 1, 2025.

Archetype 04

The Outsourcer

Seventeen, capable, busy, and quietly handing over the hard parts. The chatbot picks the outfit, drafts the email to the teacher, coaches the texts to a girlfriend, and finally writes the breakup. Nothing is wrong, exactly. The question is what becomes of a kid who never has to find the words.

Based onAn Arkansas high schooler who had an AI companion write the text ending his two-year relationship. “It felt a little bit dystopian,” his friend Ganesh Nair, 18, told the AP, “that a computer generated the end to a real relationship.” Another teen, Bruce Perry, 17, asks chatbots what to wear and has them write his emails to teachers.

Impact campaign

The film is the conversation starter

Social Machines is built to help parents and teenagers have meaningful, non-combative conversations about AI companions. The film opens the door. The impact campaign gives families and communities a way through it.

01

Community screenings

We’ll partner with The Anxious Generation Movement (TAG), cofounded by Jonathan Haidt, and other organizations on community screenings with guided discussion.

02

A small-group curriculum

We’ll collaborate with leaders in the field on a small-group curriculum that prompts honest conversation in school and church settings.

03

Conversations at home

Question-first prompts for parents and teens, written in the same empathetic, non-judgmental spirit as the film.

Casting

We start with the pattern, then find the person

Just like Social Animals, we begin with archetypes and go looking for the real, living, breathing teenager who fits the pattern.

In Social Animals the archetypes were the beauty influencer (a young starlet building a huge following), the escape artist (using the platform as a way out of hardship) and the undiscerning consumer (pulled into the world inside the app until they lose perspective). We found Kaylyn, Humza and Emma, and their real lives took the film places no outline could.
